ABSTRACT
World Action Models (WAMs) combine visual dynamics modeling with action
generation, but their high inference latency limits responsive robot control. Recent
efforts accelerate inference by removing explicit future-video generation at test
time, as in FastWAM, an approach that requires a specially tailored architectural
design. More general caching strategies exploit feature redundancy, but redun-
dancy alone does not capture the changing computational demands of closed-loop
control. To address these challenges, we present RealtimeW AM, a general, training-
free framework that coordinates parallel execution with adaptive computation for
low-latency inference across diverse WAM architectures. We exploit layerwise
dependencies to overlap observation processing with prediction. However, con-
current branches still compete for GPU resources, limiting the benefit of parallel
execution. We therefore adapt computation throughout the pipeline through se-
lective reuse, caching observation features in visually stable regions and reusing
Transformer residuals while reserving additional refinement for small predicted
adjustments. We evaluate RealtimeWAM on FastWAM and OpenWAM across
RoboTwin, LIBERO, and LIBERO-Plus. On an RTX 4090, measured mean in-
ference latencies are 24.09 and 63.09 ms, corresponding to average speedups of
8.90× and 10.67×. Average success rates are 82.75% and 87.41%, respectively,
within 0.02 and 0.53 percentage points of native inference. Across five real-world
tasks, RealtimeW AM improves average success rates over native inference by 17.2
and 37.2 percentage points on FastWAM and OpenWAM, respectively. Project
page:https://anonymous.4open.science/w/realtimewam/.

can respond to changes in its environment. This limitation is particularly problematic in dynamic
manipulation, where the scene continues to evolve while the model generates an action (Black et al.,
2025b). For example, a moving object on a conveyor may shift away from its observed position
before the predicted action is executed. Reducing latency from observation to action is therefore
essential for using W AMs to control robots in real time.
Recent work has reduced inference cost through architectural simplification and computational reuse.
For example, FastW AM removes future-video generation at inference while retaining joint training
with video (Yuan et al., 2026), and diffusion caching methods such as TeaCache avoid repeated
Transformer computation by reusing residual features (Liu et al., 2025a). These advances leave
two further questions for WAM execution in real time. First, reducing computation alone does not
resolve how observation processing and prediction should be scheduled under their data dependencies
and shared hardware constraints. Second, caching decisions based on denoising similarity are not
explicitly tied to the responsiveness and precision required by the current action. Fixed evaluation
schedules likewise apply the same computational budget throughout a rollout, despite changing
execution requirements. Further acceleration therefore calls for coordinating parallel execution with
computation that adapts to the current observation and predicted motion.
To address these challenges, we present RealtimeWAM, a framework that coordinates parallel
execution with adaptive computation for existing WAM architectures without additional training.
We first exploit the layerwise dependencies between processing the current observation (Prefill) and
prediction. As each layer produces the required observation features, the corresponding prediction
computation can begin while Prefill continues (Figure 2). This allows Prefill to overlap with action
prediction and, when present, future-video generation. However, concurrent branches compete for
shared GPU compute resources, limiting the speedup achievable through overlap alone.
To ease this contention, we exploit temporal redundancy across observations to reduce the Prefill
workload. Changes in the current observation guide which tokens receive fresh computation, while
cached features supply the remaining positions. However, reducing the number of refreshed tokens
does not always reduce latency, as different token counts can fall within the same latency plateau
(Table 3, right). We use this available capacity to refresh additional tokens with larger feature changes
in the first layer, limiting reuse error at little extra latency. This reuse policy accounts for hardware
latency and reduces the workload competing with prediction while keeping observation features
current.
Token reuse reduces the cost of the first stage, but subsequent denoising still requires repeated network
evaluations. To further accelerate inference, we adapt the refresh schedule of cached Transformer
residuals according to predicted motion. A coarse action prediction provides a displacement signal
for deciding whether further refinement is needed. Motion amplitude and the effect of additional
computation vary within an episode (Figure 3), motivating a budget adapted to motion. We allocate
fewer evaluations to large predicted movements to favor rapid replanning, while retaining additional
evaluations for small adjustments. We evaluate this allocation through closed-loop ablations in
Section 4.4 and paired prediction comparisons in Appendix E. Refinement continues from the same
initial denoising trajectory, preserving the computation already performed.

Our main contributions are:
• We design a parallel schedule guided by layer dependencies that overlaps observation
processing with action prediction and, where applicable, future-video generation.
• We develop a token reuse policy that reduces the Prefill workload and exploits hardware
latency plateaus to refresh additional informative tokens.
• We adapt Transformer residual caching to predicted motion, using the displacement of a
coarse action prediction to determine whether further refinement is needed.
• We evaluate two WAM architectures on three simulation benchmarks and five real-world
tasks, with controlled experiments examining execution, token allocation, and predictive
refinement.

3 METHOD
RealtimeW AM coordinates execution order and fresh computation within each WAM request (Fig-
ure 4). We first overlap independent work using the model’s dependencies. Hardware-aware token
reuse then reduces the observation workload competing with prediction, while motion-adaptive
refinement allocates subsequent network evaluations.
3.1 ANATOMY OFWAMINFERENCE
At request t, a W AM maps images, robot state, and an instruction to anH-step action chunk. Current-
observation processing, termedPrefill, provides features for action prediction and, when present,
joint video–action prediction. Under first-frame-causal attention and fixed clean-frame conditioning,
observation tokens are independent of the evolving prediction. Their keys and values (K/V) provide
the observation context required by the predictive branches at each layer.

The first stage includes input preparation, Prefill, and the initial prediction. Thus Rt controls the

3.2 DEPENDENCY-AWARE PARALLEL EXECUTION
We overlap independent input preparation: V AE encoding produces visual latents while context
preparation produces instruction features and conditioning K/V , reusing instruction features when
unchanged.
Prediction consumes observation K/V layer by layer. We release them immediately after projection,
allowing prediction to proceed alongside the remaining Prefill attention and FFN operations (Figure 2).
This overlaps observation processing with the first predictive evaluation, including the future-video
branch when present.
Within each layer, local projections run concurrently; joint attention waits for the required K/V , after
which conditioning attention and FFNs resume. Fused operators, preallocated buffers, and CUDA
Graph replay reduce transfers, repeated assembly, and launch overhead.
The overlapping branches share GPU resources, so we reduce their concurrent workload by refreshing
selected observation positions and reusing cached outputs at the others.

3.4 MOTION-ADAPTIVE REFINEMENT
Repeated Transformer evaluations remain on the critical path after the first stage. We cache the
input-to-output residual of the Transformer. Each denoising step embeds the current latent, adds the
cached residual, and evaluates the output head with the current timestep. Full evaluations refresh
this residual. Motivated by the varying precision demands in Figure 3, we use predicted motion
amplitude to allocate refinement: large motions favor earlier replanning, while small adjustments
receive additional computation.

4 EXPERIMENTS
We evaluate RealtimeW AM on simulation and real-world manipulation tasks, and ablate execution
optimizations, token reuse, and motion-adaptive refinement.
4.1 EXPERIMENTAL SETUP
Backbones, benchmarks, and baselines.We evaluate FastW AM and OpenW AM on RoboTwin 2.0
clean/randomized (Chen et al., 2025), LIBERO (Liu et al., 2023), and LIBERO-Plus (Fei et al., 2026).
Each backbone’s native inference is the reference; VLA-Cache (Xu et al., 2025), TeaCache (Liu et al.,
2025a), ToCa (Zou et al., 2025), and BAC (Ji et al., 2026a) provide training-free caching baselines. We
enable compilation and CUDA Graph replay for the caching baselines and RealtimeW AM. Baselines
retain their original execution schedules; RealtimeW AM adds the proposed parallel execution. Table 1
reports success rates per setting and equally weighted averages over the four settings. LIBERO
category results appear in Appendix B.4.
Metrics and timing.We report success rate and full-request latency, with speedup relative to native
inference. Runtime measurements use BF16, batch size one, and a modified 48 GiB RTX 4090.
Timing covers the prepared CPU image/state interface through denormalized CPU actions, including
input processing, model and sampler execution, transfers, and synchronization. Compilation, graph
capture, warmup, profiling, and robot execution are excluded. 2F/4F denote two/four full Transformer
evaluations within ten Euler updates. The main comparison and execution ablations report mean
latencies measured during complete closed-loop rollouts. Model-specific interfaces and checks appear
in Appendix C.

4.2 OVERALL COMPARISON
Success–latency tradeoff.RealtimeW AM has the lowest reported average latency on both backbones
(Table 1). FastWAM latency decreases from 214.33 to 24.09 ms (8.90 ×), while the success rate
changes from 82.77% to 82.75%. OpenW AM latency decreases from 672.83 to 63.09 ms (10.67×),
with the success rate changing from 87.94% to 87.41%. Relative to Native with compilation and
CUDA Graph replay, speedups are 4.14×on FastW AM and 7.15×on OpenW AM (Table 9).
Comparison with caching baselines.With compilation and CUDA Graph replay, TeaCache is the
fastest competing baseline at 49.28/245.99 ms on FastW AM/OpenW AM, with lower average success
rates than ours on both. BAC retains a higher OpenWAM success rate (87.77% versus 87.41%) at
305.08 ms. Appendix C.3 compares original and compiled execution for each baseline.
4.3 SOURCES OF LATENCY REDUCTION
Table 2 adds execution optimizations at fixed evaluation budgets: first-forward timings come from 2F
runs, and complete requests from separate 10F runs. After graph replay and compilation, parallel
execution reduces first-forward latency from 36.21 to 17.77 ms on FastWAM and from 60.62 to
37.12 ms on OpenWAM. Token reuse further reduces these to 17.69 and 31.62 ms; its incremental
benefit is larger on OpenW AM in this configuration.
Request latency with 10F falls from 214.33 to 29.47 ms on FastWAM and 672.83 to 217.24 ms on
OpenW AM, establishing execution savings before adaptive evaluation counts are introduced. Detailed
stage and preparation measurements appear in Appendix C.

5 CONCLUSION
RealtimeWAM combines parallel execution, hardware-aware token reuse, and motion-adaptive
refinement in a training-free framework. On FastW AM and OpenW AM, measured average speedups
reach 8.90× and 10.67×, with success rates within 0.02 and 0.53 percentage points of native inference.
Across five real-world tasks, average success rates improve by 17.2 and 37.2 percentage points.

D TOKENREUSE ANDSELECTION
D.1 CACHE STATE AND FEATURE CORRESPONDENCE
The observation cache stores ungated FFN outputs for 120 positions at each layer. Layer 0 is dense;
layers 1–29 share a refresh set. Attention uses current features at every position, and current residual
gates apply to fresh and cached FFN outputs. Future-video and action branches are complete at every
requested evaluation. Selected cache entries update; others retain their last refreshed outputs. The
layer-0 selector reference advances at each observation. Episode boundaries reset history; changes to
weights, conditioning, camera layout, or preprocessing require compatible cache state or full refresh.
We compare 14 observation pairs from one FastW AM trajectory and 44 pairs from four OpenW AM
adjust_bottle trajectories, eight environment steps apart. FFN change at each token is the
channelwise mean absolute or L2 difference between dense features. Mean within-frame RGB/FFN-
MAE correlations are 0.827/0.817 for FastWAM/OpenWAM at layer 0 and 0.716/0.701 across

threshold; rotation and gripper coordinates do not enter this score.
