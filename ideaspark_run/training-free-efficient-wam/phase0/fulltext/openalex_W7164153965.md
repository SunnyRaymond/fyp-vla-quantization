## Intro

Robot control requires understanding how the physical scene will evolve during interaction. World-Action Models (WAMs)

[

, bringing the computational cost of high-fidelity prediction into the control loop. The resulting latency and hardware demands make real-time deployment challenging.
A different picture is taking shape. Photorealistic future prediction is not always necessary for effective control. For example, VPP
[
14
]
shows that action generation remains effective even when the denoising process is reduced to a single step, while Fast-WAM
[
37
]
demonstrates that WAMs can remain competitive even when explicit future generation is skipped during inference. Such tolerance to reduced video computation suggests room to rethink the design of the future predictor itself. We therefore propose Efficient-WAM (Fig.

), asking whether a streamlined video branch can retain useful future guidance even at reduced visual fidelity.
Our core idea is to make the video branch smaller and more efficient within a Mixture-of-Transformers (MoT) framework
[
1
,
18
]
through structured pruning and knowledge transfer from the foundation model WAN-2.2-5B
[
26
]
. The goal is to bring the benefits of large-scale video pretraining to a smaller model that can run within a robot’s inference budget. Once this knowledge has been transferred to a compact backbone, two complementary accelerations follow naturally. First, what the robot observes and what it imagines need not have the same visual fidelity. We keep current observations at high resolution while allowing future predictions to be coarser and less costly. Second, imagining the future need not take as many sampling steps as refining an action. A short video schedule supplies features for continued action refinement, reducing the cost of repeated video prediction. Together, these choices make future imagination less expensive while retaining its role in action generation.
Early-exit and dynamic layer-skipping methods
[
38
,
32
]
mainly reduce policy-backbone computation, while single-step denoising and diffusion-policy distillation methods
[

accelerate action sampling. By contrast, we develop a unified WAM framework that systematically addresses three factors governing video-side inference cost: model size, visual-token count, and denoising steps. The ablations measure their individual effects, and real-world deployment evaluates the combined design. Together, these results establish that a future branch can be compressed across all three dimensions while retaining useful guidance for control.
Efficient-WAM denotes the compact structural baseline, while Efficient-WAM-RT denotes its real-time deployment variant with all three efficiency mechanisms enabled. We evaluate both variants in simulation and Efficient-WAM-RT on real-world tasks. Efficient-WAM achieves 86.7% clean and 85.7% randomized success on RoboTwin 2.0. Efficient-WAM-RT achieves 65.0% success across five real-world tasks, compared with 64.0% for Motus. It runs at 98 ms per chunk on an RTX 4090, over 30

•
We identify costly future prediction as a critical deployment bottleneck of WAMs. Furthermore, our compact future-imagination design shows that effective control can be decoupled from the pursuit of photorealistic video generation.
•
We decompose video-side inference cost into three factors and accordingly propose Efficient-WAM, combining a compact MoT with structured slicing and distillation, sparse future tokens, and training-free asymmetric denoising. Simulation and real-world experiments validate its efficiency and control performance.

3.2
Compact Architecture with World-Knowledge Transfer
Large video generators learn rich spatiotemporal priors, but their size makes direct use in low-latency robot control difficult. We transfer these priors from WAN-2.2-5B into a compact video expert through structured initialization and distillation. The aim is to retain the benefits of video pretraining for action generation in a much smaller model. We pair the compact video expert with a dedicated action expert in a Mixture-of-Transformers (MoT) architecture.
To build the 0.8B video expert, we reduce WAN-2.2-5B from 30 to 12 transformer layers and narrow each layer. For depth reduction, we retain the teacher layers used as anchors for two distillation losses. One matches hidden states, and the other matches temporal feature changes. 3.3
Coarse Future Prediction with Multiscale Latents
Predicting future frames at the resolution of the current observation creates a dense video-token sequence. Our framework reduces this cost with a multiscale video-latent layout. Each chunk uses the latest observation, with no visual memory carried across chunks. In all experiments, the current observation is encoded at

During inference, the current latent remains a fixed input condition, while future latents are initialized from noise. Joint video-action attention operates on this multiscale sequence. The layout reduces future-token count and attention cost while preserving the input resolution of the current observation.
3.4
Asymmetric Video-Action Denoising
Joint video-action denoising runs both experts at every sampling step. The video branch accounts for much of this computation. We allocate separate video and action budgets to reduce video forward passes while retaining more action-refinement steps.
We use training-free asymmetric denoising with independent video and action schedules. For a two-step video budget and a five- or ten-step action budget, we place the two joint updates at the first two action iterations. The video schedule spans the full noise-to-data interval in two steps. Each joint forward caches the video keys and values used at every MoT layer. After the second update, only the action stream runs: its queries attend to cached video keys/values and the current action keys/values. The cache contains intermediate denoiser features, is refreshed at each joint update, and is reset for each new action chunk. No video forward passes are needed during the remaining action steps.
